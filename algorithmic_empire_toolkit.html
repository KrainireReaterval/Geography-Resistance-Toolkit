01 — The Problem

What Is an Algorithmic Empire?

Empire has always required systems of classification. Colonial administrators mapped bodies, territories, and populations — sorting people into categories of belonging and threat. Today, those same logics live inside code.

Ruha Benjamin's concept of the "New Jim Code" describes how technologies that appear neutral actually perpetuate and expand racial hierarchies. Virginia Eubanks, in Automating Inequality, documents how algorithmic systems concentrate surveillance on communities already most harmed by institutional power. What we call "algorithmic empire" names this pattern: the use of data infrastructure to enact extraction, sorting, and control at scale.

Facial recognition, predictive policing software, biometric border systems, and training datasets built from scraped medical records — these are not separate technologies. They are components of a shared architecture, one that has predecessors in census rolls, redlining maps, and stop-and-frisk policies.

"The tech industry is not solving discrimination. It is, in many ways, exporting it."

This toolkit draws on critical geography, science and technology studies, and community organizing traditions to map how this architecture works — and where it can be interrupted. Refusal, audit, and collective pressure are all on the table.

We are not interested in making surveillance more equitable. We are interested in making it legible enough to dismantle.

02 — The Stakes

Who Gets Watched?

Surveillance is not distributed equally. Research consistently shows that biometric and predictive systems are deployed most intensively against communities marked by race, immigration status, income, and geography. Below, six axes of disproportion — each backed by documented cases.

High

Black + Brown Communities

Face recognition is up to 100× more likely to misidentify dark-skinned women. NIST audits confirm error rates diverge sharply by race and gender.

High

Undocumented Immigrants

ICE partnerships with local police and license plate reader networks create surveillance infrastructures spanning municipal boundaries — without democratic oversight.

High

Low-Income Households

Eubanks documents how welfare and housing systems subject poor families to algorithmic oversight invisible to middle-class recipients of government aid.

Med–High

Muslim + Arab Communities

Post-9/11 surveillance programs disproportionately targeted mosques, Arab-American community organizations, and international students through both federal and local programs.

Med

Political Dissidents + Organizers

From Standing Rock to Black Lives Matter, social media monitoring and drone surveillance have been used to track and suppress political organizing.

Emerging

Bodies in Dermatological AI

Medical imaging datasets are dominated by Fitzpatrick Scale I–III skin tones, creating diagnostic blind spots for darker-skinned patients — a less visible but consequential form of biometric exclusion.

Case Study — First-Person Research

The Beijing AR Lab: When Surveillance is a Research Environment

As a research assistant at a Beijing Institute of Technology laboratory working on augmented reality systems, I encountered camera-scan algorithms developed explicitly for law enforcement deployment. The workflow normalized in research culture — prototype, optimize, hand off — obscures the endpoint: a tool calibrated to identify and classify bodies in public space. The lab operated within a governance context where the ethics review process for such technologies was effectively bypassed by national security framing. This is not unique to China. The pipeline from academic computer vision to immigration enforcement, from university dataset collection to border surveillance, operates similarly in the United States. The research context is not separate from the carceral one.

03 — Issue Areas

Five Sites of Struggle

Each issue area below follows the same structure: what it is, why it matters, and what communities can do about it. Click to expand.

What It Is

Automated collection of physiological identifiers

Facial geometry, gait, iris scans, and voiceprints are captured by cameras in airports, stadiums, transit hubs, schools, and workplaces — often without explicit consent or visible signage.

Why It Matters

Bodies become permanent searchable records

Unlike passwords, biometric data cannot be changed. Once extracted and stored, it can be queried, sold, or subpoenaed indefinitely. Error rates compound the harm: false matches create legal jeopardy for the innocent.

What You Can Do

Refuse, opt out, organize

Opt out of biometric screening at airports (your legal right in the U.S.). Demand your school or employer publish a biometric data inventory. Support local legislation like the Illinois BIPA or state-level bans on facial recognition in public housing.

What It Is

Algorithms that forecast crime and criminality

Systems like PredPol, ShotSpotter, and COMPAS use historical arrest data and geographic information to direct police attention or recommend sentences — encoded in a veneer of technical objectivity.

Why It Matters

Bias is laundered as math

Historical arrest data reflects historical over-policing of Black and Brown communities. Feeding that data into a model amplifies past discrimination and presents it as neutral prediction. The algorithm does not predict crime — it predicts where police will find crime if they look for it.

What You Can Do

Demand algorithmic audits

Join or support campaigns like Stop LAPD Spying or Mijente's #NoTechForICE. File public records requests for your city's predictive policing contracts. Demand that city councils require independent algorithmic impact assessments before any new tool deployment.

What It Is

Skewed training data shapes who AI can "see"

AI systems learn from datasets. When those datasets are racially homogeneous — as most dermatological, medical imaging, and face recognition training sets are — the resulting models perform worse on excluded populations.

Why It Matters — SkinGraph

Diagnostic AI has a skin tone problem

Dermatological AI datasets overwhelmingly represent Fitzpatrick Scale I–III (lighter skin tones). SkinGraph is a knowledge graph project mapping these representation gaps — surfacing not only which conditions are under-represented, but which bodies are structurally excluded from the AI diagnostic pipeline.

What You Can Do

Audit, contribute, demand transparency

Support organizations building inclusive datasets. Demand that hospitals and health systems publishing AI tools disclose training data demographics. Ask your dermatologist or insurer what AI-assisted tools they use — and what their performance looks like across skin tones.

What It Is

Your data lives outside your jurisdiction

Cloud infrastructure, app data, and biometric records routinely cross national borders — landing in jurisdictions with weaker privacy protections, or with government access requirements that override user agreements.

Why It Matters

Diaspora communities face compounded risk

For people living between two or more state jurisdictions — immigrants, dual citizens, transnational workers — data collected by one government may be accessible to another through data-sharing agreements, surveillance partnerships, or legal compulsion. This is not hypothetical: Customs and Border Protection has accessed commercial data brokers without warrants.

What You Can Do

Practice data minimalism; support policy

Use encrypted messaging (Signal). Audit which apps have access to your location and contacts. Support legislation requiring data localization or cross-border data transfer restrictions. Advocate for Indigenous data sovereignty frameworks — models like CARE and OCAP that assert community control over population data.

What It Is

Universities, employers, and landlords buy surveillance

Facial recognition is being installed in residence halls, workplaces, and schools under the guise of security and efficiency. Procurement decisions are rarely subject to democratic input or transparency requirements.

Why It Matters

Consent is manufactured, not given

When your university deploys biometric door access "for your convenience," declining it means losing access to your own building. Coerced consent is not consent. Institutional deployment also normalizes surveillance for the next generation — acclimating people to monitoring as a condition of belonging.

What You Can Do

Organize, demand, walkout

Form a student or worker privacy coalition. File public records requests on surveillance contracts. Demand that your institution adopt an AI procurement policy requiring independent civil liberties review. Use graduate student union bargaining to include surveillance restrictions in contracts. If refused: organize collective refusal.

04 — Tactics

Rated by Strength

Not all resistance is equal. Below, tactics are rated by the scale of impact they can achieve — from individual protective action to structural intervention. The goal is not to romanticize any single level, but to understand the full ladder.

Protective

Opt out & encrypt

Decline biometric screening at airports. Use Signal. Enable full-disk encryption. Minimize app permissions. These protect you, but do not change the system.

Individual
Visible

Public records requests

Use FOIA and state equivalents to expose surveillance contracts, algorithmic audit reports, and data-sharing agreements. Forces disclosure; builds public record for organizing.

Individual + Group
Structural

Algorithmic impact campaigns

Demand that your city, university, or employer commission and publish independent algorithmic impact assessments. Tie any new surveillance procurement to mandatory review and community comment.

Coalition
Legislative

City + state bans on facial recognition

San Francisco, Boston, and others have passed municipal bans. Replicable anywhere. Requires building a multi-stakeholder coalition and a campaign infrastructure — but the policy precedent exists.

Policy + Campaign
Strongest

Collective refusal + contract campaigns

Workers at Google, Amazon, and Microsoft have organized to cancel government surveillance contracts. Graduate student unions have bargained surveillance restrictions into collective agreements. Organized collective refusal changes the cost calculus for vendors and institutions.

Your action this week:
one concrete thing

Resistance is not a single heroic act — it is accumulated decisions, documented demands, and organized relationships. Here are three entry points, pick one:

A. If you are a student: Find out whether your university uses facial recognition, biometric door access, or proctoring AI. File a public records request or email the CIO. Document what you find. Share it.
B. If you are a researcher: Audit the training data demographics of an AI system in your field. Who is included? Who is absent? What are the downstream clinical, legal, or social consequences? Write it up — even one page.
C. If you are a community member: Attend one city council meeting where surveillance procurement is on the agenda. Bring two people with you. Speak during public comment — or just show up. Presence is data too.
